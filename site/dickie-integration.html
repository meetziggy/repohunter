RepoHunter is the evaluation engine for another working project: Dickie uses it to judge open-source patterns against Dickie's own constraints before anyone reaches for a rewrite, a dependency, or a framework.

What Dickie is

Dickie—also known as agent-forge—is Brian Gorzelic's private, local, Claude-Code-based multi-agent orchestration harness. Its job is practical coordination, and its shape is deliberately boring: a bash-and-Python harness, not a destination for an entire third-party framework.

That makes the useful question narrower than “is this repository good?” Dickie needs to know whether a project contains a portable pattern worth learning from, whether that pattern fits its constraints, and whether adopting the idea is cheaper and clearer than building it again.

How the integration works

01 · PROFILE

Describe the real project

A Dickie-specific project profile records its constraints and the kinds of reusable patterns it is looking for.

02 · EVALUATE

Check a candidate

repohunter.py evaluate <owner/repo> examines a repository through that project-specific lens.

03 · DECIDE

Get a usable dossier

The result is a structured GO / MAYBE / SKIP decision about fit, tradeoffs, and the right reuse move.

The evaluation runs locally through Ollama, so Dickie can repeat the check without an API bill. The integration does not recreate RepoHunter's engine inside agent-forge; it uses RepoHunter as the tool built for this decision.

Reuse-first, applied to the reuse tool itself. Dickie supplies its context. RepoHunter supplies the evaluation. Local Ollama supplies the model backend. Each piece keeps its own job.

From research session to repeatable check

Earlier evaluations of owainlewis/neo ↗, smtg-ai/claude-squad ↗, and disler/super-simple-software-factory ↗ required hours of manual research to find portable orchestration patterns. Those projects and their authors remain the sources of the ideas; RepoHunter helps make the fit assessment repeatable for future candidates.

That attribution matters. RepoHunter's ground rule is simple: promote authors, never strip them. Evaluation should lead back to the people and repositories that produced the work—not blur their contribution into somebody else's system.
